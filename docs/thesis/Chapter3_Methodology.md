## 3.1 Chapter Overview

This chapter explains the reasoning behind how the research was conducted rather than what it
produced. It begins with the philosophical stance that frames the whole investigation, then
sets out the broad approach, the specific strategy, and the mechanisms used to gather facts.
It presents, in tabular form, how each standard research activity was addressed in practice,
and it justifies the project-management method chosen to organise the work. The chapter closes
with the project timeline and a consideration of the ethical issues raised by building and
testing a security tool. Throughout, the guiding concern is coherence: the paradigm, approach,
strategy, and methods are chosen so that they fit one another and, together, fit the research
question posed in Chapter 1.

## 3.2 Research Paradigm

The philosophical stance behind this research is pragmatism. The study was not an attempt to
uncover a single objective truth about a natural phenomenon, as a purely positivist enquiry
might be, nor was it an attempt to interpret subjective human meaning, as an interpretivist
enquiry would. Its purpose was more direct: to find out whether a particular engineered design
*works*, and whether it works *better* than an established alternative when judged against a
clearly defined and measurable criterion. Pragmatism is the paradigm that fits this intent,
because it evaluates ideas by their practical consequences and is comfortable drawing on
whatever combination of methods best answers the question at hand.

Adopting a pragmatic stance had concrete consequences for the design of the study. It justified
the decision to build a real, running artefact and then to let experimental measurement — rather
than argument or intuition — determine whether the design achieved its aim. It also legitimised
combining two quite different sources of evidence within a single project: an existing,
independently produced labelled dataset on one hand, and measurements generated by operating the
system under controlled conditions on the other. A stricter, single-method paradigm would have
made that combination awkward to justify, whereas pragmatism treats the mixing of methods as a
strength when each contributes something the other cannot. In short, the paradigm was chosen
because the question is fundamentally a practical one, and pragmatism keeps the methodology
honestly aligned with that practicality.

## 3.3 Research Approach

The approach is primarily deductive and experimental, with an explicit design-and-build
component. The reasoning proceeded from the general to the particular. From the literature a
clear expectation was formed — in effect a hypothesis — that a two-tier, adaptive, in-kernel
design would maintain attack detection while reducing flash-crowd false positives compared with
a static threshold. The remainder of the work then tested that expectation by constructing the
system and observing whether the predicted outcome actually occurred under repeated and varied
conditions. This top-down movement, from a theory-derived prediction to its empirical test, is
the defining feature of a deductive approach and distinguishes it from a purely inductive study
that would instead build generalisations upward from unstructured observation.

At the same time, the object of study did not exist before the research began; it had to be
created in order to be examined. For that reason the approach also draws on design-science
thinking, in which the constructed artefact is itself a legitimate part of the contribution and
knowledge is produced through the act of building and evaluating it. The two views are
complementary here: design science accounts for the creation of Cerberus, and the deductive,
experimental view accounts for how the resulting artefact was tested against a baseline.

## 3.4 Research Strategy

The strategy combines design (the construction of the Cerberus prototype) with controlled
experimentation (the systematic measurement of that prototype). Experimentation is the
appropriate strategy here for a specific reason: the research question is comparative, and the
relevant variables can be deliberately controlled. In a controlled experiment the investigator
holds most conditions constant and changes only the factor under study, so that any observed
difference in outcome can reasonably be attributed to that factor rather than to chance or to
some uncontrolled difference in the inputs.

In practice this meant running the same, repeatable traffic scenarios against two configurations
of the same system — a static-threshold baseline representing the conventional approach, and the
adaptive two-tier configuration representing the proposed design — while keeping the generated
traffic, the host, and the measurement procedure identical between the two runs. Because only the
configuration differed, a difference in the number of legitimate users wrongly blocked could be
attributed to the configuration itself. This design also made the results reproducible: the same
scenario can be run again and is expected to yield the same qualitative outcome, which is a basic
requirement of credible experimental work.

## 3.5 Fact-Collection Mechanisms

Three distinct sources of fact were used, and using more than one gave the evaluation more than
one independent footing. The first source was the **CIC-IDS-2017 dataset**, a widely used and
independently produced record of labelled benign and attack network flows. It was used to train
and to test the Tier-2 decision tree offline, before any live traffic was involved, so that the
classifier's quality could be assessed against data the project did not itself create. Using an
established benchmark rather than a self-generated dataset strengthens the credibility of the
machine-learning results, because the data were not shaped to flatter the model.

The second source was **direct measurement from the running system**. With the prototype attached
to the kernel, controlled attack and flash-crowd traffic was generated and directed at the host,
and the per-source counters, the global drop and pass tallies, and the classifier's verdicts were
read straight from the kernel maps and the system's records. These measurements are the primary
evidence for the central claim, because they capture what the system actually did to real
traffic rather than what it was predicted to do. A deliberate methodological choice was made to
read the kernel maps directly rather than through the network interface, so that the measurement
channel could not itself be affected by the filtering under test.

The third and more informal source was the **behaviour observed during development**, which fed
back into design decisions. Several practical facts — for example how a particular kernel reacted
to the program, or how loopback traffic behaved under a low threshold — could only be discovered
by running the system, and these observations shaped subsequent choices. Taken together, the
labelled dataset, the live measurements, and the developmental observations provided a fuller and
more robust body of evidence than any one of them alone.

## 3.7 Project Management Methodology

The work was managed in an agile, iterative style rather than as a single large build carried out
in one pass. This choice suited the project for a concrete and important reason. With eBPF, a
significant part of the system's behaviour cannot be predicted reliably in advance: whether the
verifier will accept a given program, how a particular kernel version will react to it, and
whether a network interface supports native XDP are all matters that, in practice, are discovered
by trying rather than by planning. A method that front-loads all planning and defers all testing —
a strict waterfall, for example — copes badly with that kind of uncertainty, because a problem
discovered late can invalidate a great deal of earlier work. An iterative method, in which each
short cycle produces something that actually runs and is tested before the next cycle begins,
absorbs the uncertainty far more gracefully.

The project was therefore organised as a sequence of small, individually runnable milestones. The
progression moved deliberately from the simplest possible foundation upward: a minimal program
that merely attaches at the XDP hook and passes all traffic, confirming that the toolchain and the
machine can load eBPF at all; then a single global packet counter, confirming that kernel and
userspace can share state through a map; then per-source counting with thresholded dropping, which
is the core of Tier 1; then the userspace read layer, the database, the API, and the dashboard;
then the adaptive control loop; then the machine-learning tier; and finally the evaluation. Each
milestone was verified before the next was begun, so that at no point did a large, untested body
of code accumulate. This organisation is closest in spirit to the short, outcome-bearing
iterations of Scrum, scaled down to a single-person project, and it proved well matched to the
exploratory nature of kernel-level development.

### 3.7.2 Ethical Considerations

Although the project did not involve human participants, building and testing a security tool
raises ethical points that were considered and addressed directly. All attack and flash-crowd
traffic used in the study was synthetic and was generated against a machine under the researcher's
own control, on an isolated setup; at no stage was any third-party system, network, or service
targeted, so no live service was placed at risk and no unauthorised testing took place. Because no
personal or human-subject data was collected, questions of informed consent and privacy did not
arise. The one external asset used, the CIC-IDS-2017 dataset, is a recognised research dataset
that was used within its intended terms and cited appropriately. Finally, mindful that the
techniques involved can in principle be misused, the write-up concentrates on the defensive design
and its evaluation and deliberately avoids presenting material whose main value would be to assist
an attacker. These measures keep the research within responsible bounds while still allowing a
meaningful evaluation to be carried out.

## 3.8 Chapter Summary

This chapter set out how the research was conducted and why those choices were appropriate. It
adopted a pragmatic paradigm, suited to a question about whether an engineered design works better
than a baseline, and a deductive, experimental approach with a design-and-build component,
appropriate to a study whose object had to be constructed before it could be measured. Facts were
drawn from three complementary sources — the CIC-IDS-2017 dataset, direct measurement of the
running prototype, and observations made during development — and the work was managed as a series
of small, individually tested milestones in order to cope with the uncertainty inherent in
kernel-level eBPF development. The ethical footprint of the work was small and was handled by using
only self-controlled targets, synthetic traffic, and a properly licensed dataset. The chapters
that follow apply this methodology in practice: Chapter 4 specifies the system's requirements,
Chapter 5 describes its implementation, and Chapter 6 reports and reviews the evaluation.
